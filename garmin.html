Visiting Garmin HQ in Grey Lynn

What I learned about how a global hardware company actually builds things.

I did not know what to expect walking into the Garmin office in Grey Lynn. Garmin is one of those companies you interact with without really thinking about GPS devices, marine radios, smartwatches. But what goes into building those products is a lot more complex than I ever thought about until I spent a morning there.

The visit was focused on their marine division specfically media which handles radios and sound systems for boats. What struck me immediately was how different the problems they solve are from the software world I am used to. It's not just about writing good code, their products have to survive saltwater, extreme temperatures, vibrations on different vessels and physical drops. Garmin are more focused on the physical aspects like the durability of their products more than just the software. They test everything: salt water exposure, temperature cycling from extreme highs and lows, pressure testing and acoustic testing. What I found quite cool and unique is their quality assurance guy whose entire job is to sit and listen to how their speakers sound. He is an ex-musician and has a great attention to detail when it comes to sound quality. I thought what I cool job, and he most definetly enjoys it. It is a reminder that engineering is not just about code, it is about the end product and how it is experienced by the user.

How they build products

One of the things I found most interesting was learning about how Garmin manages product development across their different divisions. They work from detailed product plans that map out everything engineers and software developers need to deliver. When one team builds something useful, other divisions sometimes spark interest and want to use it too. I spoke to someone who I believe described how software they built for a radio ended up being adapted for a smartwatch after engineers in another part of the company reached out asking how they had done it. That kind of internal collaboration across a global company is something I had never really thought about before. It very cool to see how different deparetments acros the world can work together to build better products.

They code primarily in C# and C++, which makes sense given the low level hardware integration required. A lot of their testing happens in house now after they recently moved into a new facility, which means they can make incremental changes and test them immediately rather than shipping hardware overseas and waiting weeks for feedback. A lot of their testing is physical instead of just software based, which is a different approach to what I am used to. They have a lot of specialised equipment for testing and quality assurance, which was really interesting to see in action. I had to ask about AI and how it is being used in their products, and they said that it definetly helps to streamline the development process but not going to takeover. It was cool to see how they are using AI to improve their products and make them more efficient in the development process.

What I took from it

Both of the younger engineers I spoke to had come in as interns and received return offers. That reinforced something I keep hearing: internships are the pathway. You get in, you show what you can do and if you are good the door stays open.

The other thing that hit me was how much engineering goes into products that people use without a second thought. A marine radio sounds simple, in reality it has to handle connectivity across Bluetooth, Airplay and traditional analogue inputs. It has to survive conditions most consumer electronics would not last a day in and keep up with shifting connectivity standards like the transition from USB-A to USB-C. There is a lot of careful, unglamorous work behind things that just work.

Garmin was a great visit and it gave me a much clearer picture of what hardware-adjacent software engineering actually looks like in practice. It reminded me that the problems worth solving are rarely as simple as they appear from the outside. The people I spoke to were approachable and willing to share their rather private experiences, which made it a really valuable part of the day. A special thanks to the Garmin team for hosting me and the UOA IEEE student branch for organising the visit. I would highly recommend any student to take the opportunity to visit a company like this if they get the chance.
