Visiting Garmin HQ in Grey Lynn

What I learned about how a global hardware company actually builds things.

I did not know what to expect walking into the Garmin office in Grey Lynn. Garmin is one of those companies you interact with without really thinking about it: GPS devices, marine radios, smartwatches. But what goes into building those products is a lot more complex than I had ever considered until I spent a morning there.

The visit focused on their marine division, specifically media, which handles radios and sound systems for boats. What struck me immediately was how different the problems they solve are from the software world I am used to. It is not just about writing good code. Their products have to survive saltwater, extreme temperatures, vibration on different vessels and physical drops.

Garmin focus as much on the physical side, like the durability of their products, as on the software. They test everything: saltwater exposure, temperature cycling between extreme highs and lows, pressure testing and acoustic testing.

What I found cool and unique was their quality assurance engineer whose entire job is to sit and listen to how their speakers sound. He is an ex-musician with a great ear for sound quality. I thought what a cool job, and he definitely enjoys it. It is a reminder that engineering is not just about code. It is about the end product and how the user experiences it.

How they build products

One of the most interesting things was learning how Garmin manages product development across its divisions. They work from detailed product plans that map out everything engineers and software developers need to deliver. When one team builds something useful, other divisions sometimes take an interest and want to use it too.

I spoke to someone who, I believe, described how software built for a radio ended up being adapted for a smartwatch after engineers in another part of the company reached out asking how they had done it. That kind of internal collaboration across a global company is something I had never really thought about. It was very cool to see how departments around the world work together to build better products.

They code primarily in C# and C++, which makes sense given the low level hardware integration required. Much of their testing now happens in house after a recent move into a new facility, so they can make incremental changes and test them immediately instead of shipping hardware overseas and waiting weeks for feedback. A lot of that testing is physical rather than software based, which is a different approach to what I am used to, and they have plenty of specialised equipment for it.

I had to ask about AI and how it is being used. They said it definitely helps streamline the development process but is not going to take over. It was good to see how they are using it to make development more efficient.

What I took from it

Both of the younger engineers I spoke to had come in as interns and received return offers. That reinforced something I keep hearing: internships are the pathway. You get in, you show what you can do and if you are good the door stays open.

The other thing that hit me was how much engineering goes into products people use without a second thought. A marine radio sounds simple, but in reality it has to handle connectivity across Bluetooth, AirPlay and traditional analogue inputs. It has to survive conditions most consumer electronics would not last a day in and keep up with shifting standards like the move from USB-A to USB-C. There is a lot of careful, unglamorous work behind things that just work.

Garmin was a great visit and it gave me a much clearer picture of what hardware-adjacent software engineering looks like in practice. It reminded me that the problems worth solving are rarely as simple as they appear from the outside. The people I spoke to were approachable and willing to share their experiences, which made it a really valuable morning.

A special thanks to the Garmin team for hosting us and to the UOA IEEE student branch for organising the visit. I would highly recommend any student take the opportunity to visit a company like this if they get the chance.
